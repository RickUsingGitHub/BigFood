Big Food on trial

Sugar, soft drinks, juice, sweeteners, ultra-processed food, alcohol, meat, eggs, milk, seed oils and salt. What does the best science actually say about them today? And who has spent the last sixty years paying for studies, front groups and lobbyists to muddy the answer?

The short version
The boring advice was right. The companies spent decades and fortunes muddying it.
Mostly plants, whole foods and water; go easy on sugary drinks, alcohol, processed meat and salt. Some old villains (eggs, coffee, seed oils) turned out fine. And some industry "facts" (sugar is harmless, a drink a day is good for you) were paid for.

Meanwhile, in Australia

It didn't all happen in America

A Heart Foundation Tick on McDonald's

In 2007 nine McDonald's meals carried the Heart Foundation Tick, a licence reportedly worth about A$330,000 a year. The AMA asked if the Tick had been "hamburgled". The program was retired in 2015.

Stars "as prepared", and a voluntary system that stalled

Milo rated 4.5 Health Stars because it was scored as if mixed with skim milk. After twelve voluntary years, only about 39% of eligible products carried a rating, so in 2026 ministers began making it compulsory.

Toddler "fruit" snacks that were two-thirds sugar

Heinz Little Kids Shredz, sold for 1–3-year-olds with pictures of fruit and veg, were up to 68% sugar. The Federal Court fined Heinz A$2.25 million in 2018.

The "Australian Paradox"

A 2011 University of Sydney paper said Australians were eating less sugar while getting fatter, a claim the sugar industry loved. An economist spent years showing the data didn't support it; corrections followed.

Sydney's buried diet trial

The Sydney Diet Heart Study (1966–73) swapped butter for safflower oil, and more men died. The full data weren't recovered until 2013. Seed-oil sceptics still cite it; the trial margarine may have contained trans fats.

"Free range" hens that never saw outside

The ACCC won penalties against egg producers over fake free-range claims. The national standard then set "free range" at up to 10,000 hens per hectare, far denser than consumer groups wanted.

06The verdict

What the evidence agrees on, which scares don't hold up, and whether the food and drink industry is getting any better.

What the science agrees on

  • Sugary drinks are the clearest single diet–disease link: weight, type 2 diabetes, teeth.
  • Alcohol causes cancer from low levels. The "glass of red for your heart" was mostly a statistical illusion.
  • Processed meat causes bowel cancer. The risk is real but modest; make it occasional.
  • Too much salt raises blood pressure and strokes; a large trial proved it.
  • Diets heavy in ultra-processed food make people overeat. Which ingredients or processes are to blame is still being worked out.
  • Whole grains, fibre, fruit, vegetables, legumes and nuts are consistently linked to longer, healthier lives.

Scares that don't hold up

  • Seed oils aren't toxic. Swapping butter for them lowers heart risk.
  • Eggs are fine for most people at up to one a day.
  • Coffee went from suspect to one of the most consistently "protective" drinks.
  • Sweeteners don't cause cancer at normal intakes, though they don't help much with weight either.
  • Full-fat dairy isn't the heart villain it was painted as.
  • "Superfoods" like coconut oil, pomegranate juice and resveratrol pills were mostly marketing, and in one case fraud.

Is Big Food getting better? A scorecard

The bottom line

The food mostly isn't poison. The spin often is.

Most foods on this list are fine in normal amounts, and a few are good for you. The ones that consistently cause harm are sugary drinks, alcohol, processed meat and too much salt. They're also the ones whose industries have fought hardest, funding friendly science, setting up front groups, and fighting taxes and warning labels.

Is it getting better? On transparency, yes: secret deals like the 1967 sugar review would be hard to hide today, trans fats are gone, and the UK's sugar levy worked. On power, no: alcohol cancer warnings were delayed in Ireland and numeric drink limits dropped from US guidelines, and Australia still has no sugar tax and only a voluntary star rating. And confusion now comes from every direction, including wellness influencers and governments citing studies that don't exist.

The best defence is dull: be suspicious of single studies and of anything promising a miracle, check who paid, and remember the advice that hasn't changed in 50 years.

08Method & sources

What "the evidence" means here. Where randomised trials exist (salt substitutes, the NIH ultra-processed food trial, saturated fat swaps), they come first. Otherwise the site relies on large systematic reviews and pooled cohort studies, and on the World Health Organization, its cancer agency IARC, the World Cancer Research Fund, and Australia's NHMRC. Most nutrition evidence is observational, so "linked to" does not prove "causes"; certainty ratings reflect that.

The hype gap and the grid are judgement calls that summarise the cited sources on a five-point scale, at typical intakes. They're a map, not a measurement. Personal circumstances (diabetes, kidney disease, pregnancy, allergies) change the advice, so check with your GP.

Cases. Each case is described in the language of court findings, regulator decisions or the published research that exposed it. Settling is not admitting: many settlements were made without admitting liability. Jury verdicts can be cut or overturned on appeal. Amounts are in US dollars at about the exchange rate of the time.

Funding isn't proof of fraud. Industry-funded research can be excellent, and independent research can be wrong; the biggest p-hacking and fraud cases here were academics. Funding is a reason to read more carefully, not to dismiss.

Advocacy sources are labelled. Some evidence comes from groups with their own agenda (for example a vegan doctors' group on egg funding). Where that's the case, the site says so.
